🌱 Project findings & conservation action

Research question: Which of five Maharashtra cities shows the most stable reported sightings across five bird species, and how might environmental conditions relate?

What we can say

Our five selected birds and cities provide a clear framework for comparing GBIF reports.

What remains to test: Complete city–species records, observation effort, tree cover, air quality and rainfall. Until those are comparable, the dashboard intentionally does not announce a winning city or claim pollution caused a decline.

Actions citizens can take

  • Observe birds responsibly and log dated sightings.
  • Protect native trees, shrubs and nesting spaces.
  • Reduce litter and disturbance near bird habitats.
  • Share verified observations to improve biodiversity knowledge.

Presentation takeaway: Technology can help people observe nature, investigate environmental change, and take informed conservation action.

🌳 Environmental interpretation — what to test

Tree cover and green corridors

More connected native vegetation may provide nesting sites, shelter and food. Compare measured canopy cover with standardized bird trends before drawing conclusions.

Air pollution and urban density

Traffic, noise and habitat fragmentation can affect bird communities; urban-adapted pigeons and crows may respond differently from sparrows.

Rainfall and temperature

Seasonal rainfall and heat can affect food and water availability. Compare the same seasons and years across cities.

Which city is best?

No defensible ranking yet: occurrence reporting effort and environmental measurements are incomplete. A higher record count alone does not prove healthier biodiversity.

These are evidence-based hypotheses, not measured explanations of increases or declines in Mumbai, Pune, Nashik, Thane or Nagpur.

Research interpretation

Once bird occurrence records load, the highest and lowest reporting cities will appear here. This does not establish their ecological condition.

To test a claim such as “more trees support more birds,” add comparable tree-cover data for all five cities and analyze the relationship, controlling for observation effort.

Environmental dataset input

Environmental indicators are prefilled with source-backed proxies for a first-pass comparison. PM2.5 is the reported 2022 annual average (µg/m³). “Tree cover %” is actually 2023 district-wide forest-cover percentage from FSI, NOT urban tree canopy. “Rainfall” is the IMD June–September 2024 rainfall departure from normal (%), NOT rainfall depth in millimetres. Mumbai uses the simple average of Mumbai City and Mumbai Suburban departures; its forest-cover proxy combines those two district records by area. Use these as exploratory contextual indicators, not precise measurements for the city centre.

Methodology and limitations

Citizen-submitted sightings in this demo are self-reported, unverified, and stored locally; they are never included in the GBIF research counts. Each query uses the GBIF public occurrence search endpoint with a species scientific name, approximate city bounding box, calendar year, and country=IN. Results are reported occurrence records, not individual birds. Overlapping bounding boxes and uneven observer effort prevent reliable city population comparisons. A positive or negative record change cannot be claimed as a biological population increase or decline. GBIF coverage is incomplete, and live browser access may be blocked by network restrictions.

For scientific population trend conclusions, use effort-adjusted eBird Status & Trends estimates or standardized repeated surveys. Environmental causation requires measured independent variables and additional controls. The environmental table uses district-wide forest-cover percentages as a proxy for urban green cover, and IMD monsoon rainfall departures for June–September 2024; these are contextual proxies and are not directly comparable to city-centre tree canopy or rainfall totals in mm.
